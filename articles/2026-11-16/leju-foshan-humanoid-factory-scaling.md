# Inside Leju’s Foshan Plant: China Targets 10,000 Humanoid Robots a Year

![Humanoid robots in factory setting - Pixabay](https://cdn.pixabay.com/photo/2025/04/13/09/46/ai-robot-technology-9531474_640.jpg)
*Image: Free Pixabay image (Pixabay Content License) – humanoid robot in a high-tech environment.*

Leju Robotics, working with Dongfang Precision Group, has brought a 15,000-square-meter facility in Foshan, Guangdong online with a stated annual capacity of 10,000 humanoid robots. CNN received rare access to the plant in mid-November 2026, offering one of the clearest public looks yet at Chinese efforts to industrialize humanoid production. The joint venture (sometimes referred to in connection with DF Primo / Oriental Genesis Robotics) launched the line earlier in 2026 and aims to move humanoids from low-volume prototypes to consistent, high-throughput output.

### How the Line Works
The facility uses modular, flexible assembly with a high degree of automation. Reports describe roughly 24 precision assembly processes, dozens of inspection points (figures cited around 77), and extensive quality checks before any unit ships. One robot can be completed approximately every 30 minutes on the high-volume line. Material handling relies on automated guided vehicles, verified material cabinets, and digital traceability. Model changes are designed to be rapid — swapping end-effectors or joint modules and loading matching software parameters.

Not every robot is bipedal. Some models, such as wheeled variants intended for industrial settings, prioritize stability and payload over walking. Consistency is repeatedly cited as the core engineering challenge: with more than 1,000 components per unit, small variances in assembly can affect walking, grasping, or long-term reliability. Pre-shipment tests include balance, gait, stair, and strength checks under monitoring.

### Context and Implications
China already dominates much of the hardware supply chain for motors, gearboxes, sensors, and batteries. Scaling assembly capacity is therefore a logical next step. Units from Leju (including Kuavo-series models) have been delivered to automotive and appliance companies in earlier volume. Industry observers note that while hardware throughput is advancing quickly, software that generalizes across unstructured environments remains the larger remaining hurdle — a point reinforced by parallel advances in vision-language-action models elsewhere.

The Foshan plant is part of a broader wave: other Chinese and international players are also targeting thousands of units annually, safety certification frameworks are being built (e.g., LG work with Korean institutes), and pilot deployments continue in warehouses, stores, and factories. Mass production capability does not automatically equal widespread useful work, but it lowers cost curves and makes larger real-world data collection possible.

**Helpful Tip**: Track not only claimed annual capacity but actual shipped volumes, failure rates after delivery, and the mix of bipedal vs. wheeled platforms. Production rate is only one piece of the commercialization puzzle.
